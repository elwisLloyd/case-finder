Marketplace Forecasting: Sales or Demand? Why not both? Let’s find out!
Time Series forecasting is a common — but interesting — task in retail. Here at Mercado Libre, we have many projects in which we try to anticipate what the future holds. In this article, we will present to you how we predict future movements of sales and demand for the items we sell on the marketplace, so that we are able to inform a variety of internal decisions.
Before we start, we believe it is better to come to terms with what we understand as sales and demand, which in our context aren’t always interchangeable. In the retail context, sales are registered transactions performed by customers, which are limited to the available stock for each item. In contrast, by demand we refer to the potential sales if there was enough stock to meet them.
We wanted to predict the next 12 weeks of sales and demand of each individual item sold by first party sellers at Mercado Libre, in each of the countries we operate. These requirements arise from our interest in suggesting the number of units to restock for each item, taking into account that some of them have long periods of lead time.
… Enough talk! Let’s go get the juice.
Global Time Series Models
In our Marketplace, items can come and go, and so should our forecasts. We do not have a specific set of items to forecast; therefore we needed a model that works for all items without restrictions. These types of models are called Global Time Series (GTS) models, and they have proven to be the best approach to forecast many series with different behaviors. In particular, Hewamalage, H., Bergmeir, C., & Bandara, K. (2020) and Montero-Manso, P., & Hyndman, R. J. (2021) studied the performance of GTS over time series from different data generating processes, and found that the model complexity of a GTS is lower than that of multiple specific individual models. In addition, they found that tree-based models and Recurrent Neural Networks (particularly LSTM) perform better than other types of global models.
Since we need a multi-step forecasting model, we decided to use the LSTM approach as it can perform a one-shot prediction, which is better than training 12 individual tree-based models, or dealing with a lot of lags of the variables.

Fine for sales, but what about the demand?

How did it go?
Evaluating model performance is not a straightforward task. In some cases, one has to determine the appropriate metric that better represents the impact of the model in its production environment. In the GTS models, the common metrics are the Mean Absolute Error (MAE), the Mean Absolute Scaled Error (MASE), the Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) and the Symmetric Mean Absolute Percentage Error (sMAPE). Each of these metrics has advantages and disadvantages over the rest. Thus, we need to analyze each of them and then decide on the metric to use.

For more information on evaluation metrics for time series, please refer to Chapter 3.4 of the book Forecasting: Principles and Practice by Rob J Hyndman and George Athanasopoulos, which also served as inspiration for this story’s featured image.
- MAE: This metric computes the average of the absolute forecast errors of the model, and weighs them equally. It is only recommended when the time series are all in the same units. Its interpretation is an average absolute deviation in the original units.
- MAPE: It’s a measure of error relative to the magnitude of the target. This is a common and useful metric, but has the potential to divide by zero. In our case, the items have weeks in which they do not have any sales, therefore computing this metric becomes problematic. When the MAPE is non-applicable, then a better metric could be the MASE, which rarely divides by zero.
- MASE: It is a scale-free metric that is better suited for evaluating GTS from time series of different units. This metric is computed as a quotient of the MAE by the MAE of a Naïve Forecast of the series in the training set. This metric is easily interpreted as an improvement over the simplest forecast. The MASE assumes that we have time series of the same length in the training set, which is not true in our application, thus limiting the evaluation to series that have a certain length might bias our results.
- sMAPE: This is another scale-free metric based on percent errors relative to the mean between the forecast and the true value. This metric is useful for the comparison of models that forecast series with different units. This metric has three main issues: the lack of interpretability, the high skewness when either the target or the forecast value is zero, and undefinedness when both are exactly zero (so we won’t be able to measure the performance of a model that predicts zero sales when there are no actual sales).
Based on the previous analysis, the most appropriate choice to evaluate the model performance is the MAE. In particular, it doesn’t have any issues with zero sales, has a clear interpretation, and can be easily computed for every item and every model.

Items that had sales
Since our primary goal is to forecast future sales and demand of the items, we start by comparing our forecasts against the true sales of items that had at least one unit sold in the forecast horizon. The following plot presents the average MAE for each week. At first glance, it is noticeable that both our models improve over a mean forecast, while being fairly stable around 10 units of error, even at the end of the forecast horizon.
Both models seem to be adequate for predicting the next weeks, as they have lower errors than the baseline. Since these models were trained with different objectives in mind, we needed to investigate the differences between them.
In the following plot, we see that we are dealing with an item whose sales are decreasing over time, a behavior associated with a decreasing stock. Our sales model captures that trend and predicts a decrease in sales for the next 12 weeks. In contrast, our demand forecast, even though it is inaccurate, predicts that if the item had enough stock, its sales would be higher, similar to the previous levels.
Items Without Sales
When it comes to items that did not have any sales during the forecast period, the difference between the sales and demand forecasts is quite important. We want a sales forecast to predict the lowest possible sales while we want the demand forecast to predict units higher than zero. In the following plot, we present the weekly MAE of our forecasts and compare them against the mean forecast. In this plot, there is an additional model which we’ve called “Sales Forced”, meaning that we forced the prediction of the sales models to zero if the item had ran out of stock in the last week of input.
This plot reveals many interesting behaviors of both the models and the items that did not have any sales. In particular, the reason why the mean forecast has the lowest error is because items that did not have sales in the forecast horizon, didn’t have any sales in the previous 12 weeks, or at least had a very low number of them. In addition, our forced sales model has a MAE that is close to that of the mean forecast, meaning that our rule of stockout captures most of the items that did not have sales, while some others didn’t run out of stock but still had no sales and we couldn’t anticipate that.
Finally, our demand forecast exhibits an increasing error across the horizon, which at first glance seems undesirable. Nevertheless, this is everything but unexpected. Since we’ve trained our model so that it is able to create a “potential sales” forecast, it expects that each item generates sales or at least recovers part of the previous sales. On the one hand, since this particular item has run out of stock, our forced sales model predicts that it won’t have any sales during the following 12 weeks, which has turned out to be an accurate sales prediction. On the other hand, since we care about what sales could be given enough stock, our demand forecast predicts an upcoming gradual sales recovery, which didn’t occur as the item was never restocked.
Additional Notes
Since sales are not only driven by their past history since future events have an impact — positive or negative — we decided to include special events into our forecasts. These events affect the “raw” forecast in such a way that there is a special variation into the forecast value for the week of the event and the adjacent weeks. This procedure was carried out as a post-processing step, since including the special dates into our models increased errors and required a separate model for each country.
Concluding Remarks
In this article, we have guided you through the current version of our sales and demand forecasting models for the items sold at Mercado Libre. In particular, we’ve shared how to create a sales and demand forecasting model based on sales data using a Global Time Series framework, to predict upcoming sales and potential sales, respectively. We have seen that integrating both models is more effective than falling for one or the other since they have different behaviors in the forecast horizon: the sales forecast predicts a decrease in sales due to decreasing stocks, while the demand forecast predicts a sales recovery particularly for items that may run out of stock.
But… we are not done! At Mercado Libre we are always in #BetaContinuo (Continuous Beta), which means that we are always finding opportunities to try out new things and improve! Recall that the models presented above are point forecasting models, but our replenishment decisions can benefit from taking into account the uncertainty of our forecasts. Therefore, our next steps are focused on trying to find the distribution of our forecast, by either building global probabilistic forecasting models using MonteCarlo Dropout or finding the parameters of a suitable probability distribution. These improvements will enrich our forecasts and create more value for both our internal users and of course, Mercado Libre’s customers!
