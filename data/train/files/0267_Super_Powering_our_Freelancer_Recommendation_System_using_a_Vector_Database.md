Super Powering our Freelancer Recommendation System using a Vector Database
An in-depth look at how Malt improved its freelancer matching process through a retriever-ranker architecture and vector database
Malt’s mission is to connect freelancers with the perfect project. Our data team continuously works to improve the recommendation system powering these connections. In 2023, we made a significant leap forward thanks to a new architecture designed to boost the precision, speed, and scalability of our matching process. This article takes you through the development of the system, the challenges faced, and the choices behind our ‘retriever-ranker architecture’. Notably, vector databases have been critical for this solution: how we selected our technology partner and implemented it.
Our challenges
Back in 2021, we released the first version of our automatic matching between freelancers and projects, relying on a single, monolithic model. Over time, we brought more capabilities to this model, such as multilingual support, and improved its performance. However, the architecture resulted in slow response times (up to one minute!) and made it difficult to adapt for future needs and new large language models that could handle the most complex matching cases in real time. Recognizing the limitations of our model (slowness, inflexibility, etc.) led us to explore alternative solutions in 2023.
The Retriever-Ranker architecture
In our search for the most effective way to match freelancers and projects, we explored several methodologies, including cross-encoders and bi-encoders, each with its own benefits and drawbacks.
Cross-encoder: The most accurate, but too slow to scale
A cross-encoder is a neural model that processes two input texts jointly to produce a single output score or classification. It allows for complex interactions between the inputs, making it highly effective for tasks like relevance ranking or text pair classification.
When looking to measure the relevance between a freelancer and a project, our two entities, a cross-encoder model is a natural start. In this setup, there is only one model that encodes both elements simultaneously and then outputs a relevance score: on 0 to 1, how good of a match is that pair? This approach works great; the quality (precision) is really good because the model can capture complex relationships between the two entities. However, it is slow: for each project we receive (for instance, a client seeking a Java developer), we need to create a pairing with each freelancer (over 700,000 at the time of writing) and process each project-freelancer pair through the model, thus computing over 700,000 pairs!
Bi-encoder: The fastest, but less accurate
On the other hand, we can build what we call a bi-encoder: In this setup, both entities are encoded separately (using the same or two dedicated encoders / models) to produce an embedding–a vector that represents the entity in a semantic space. We can then use a similarity measure, such as cosine similarity, to compare the embeddings and obtain a relevancy score. The results of this method are not as good as the cross-encoder, as we lose the relationship between the freelancer and the project, but it is fast. Since the encodings are independent, we can precompute all the embeddings for the freelancers and then, at inference time:
- Compute only the embedding for the project (in our previous example, embed the “Java” keyword along with other project attributes).
- Apply a KNN (k-nearest neighbors) search to retrieve the top K freelancers according to our similarity measure. In practice, a KNN search is too slow when there are too many vectors (as is the case with our 700,000+ freelancers), that is why we will use what we call Approximate Nearest Neighbour (ANN) search algorithm.
Our Solution: a combination, leveraging both strengths
In our case, we aimed to find a balance between speed and accuracy, ensuring that our system could handle the scale of our platform while delivering matches of the highest quality. That is why we decided to build a two-step process called Retriever-Ranker:
- The first step (the Retriever) objective is to quickly narrow down the vast pool of freelancers to a more manageable subset relevant to the project’s description by using a bi-encoder model. This step is crucial for improving the efficiency of the overall system, as it reduces the computational load for the subsequent step. The Retriever is built for recall, ensuring that we do not miss out on potential matches, even if, at this step, we are not yet state of the art in terms of quality.
- Following the initial pre-selection, the Ranker phase kicks in. Here, the focus shifts to precision, by using a cross-encoder model where the representations of the project and freelancer are combined and fed into a network. The output is a score that reflects the degree of match between the project and the freelancer. This score is used to rank the freelancers, with the top scores indicating the most suitable candidates.
Let’s go back to our example:
- The first step, the retriever, will quickly return a relevant subset, for example, 1,000 profiles with Java expertise out of our 700,000+ freelancers. However, this subset could include less relevant profiles than others (for instance, not the exact required version of Java or not within the company’s industry).
- The second step, the ranker, will rank the 1,000 profiles and place at the top of the list the freelancers that perfectly match the project (Java version, industry etc…).
Ultimately, we decided to deploy this matching system in two phases: first by deploying a retriever that sits before our actual matching model (that acts as the ranker). This setup allows us to validate the scalability and performance of the retriever. Then we can develop a more complex ranker on top of it.
At the time of writing, our new ranker is still in development, so let’s focus on the retriever deployment.
Diving into the Retriever Architecture
At the heart of the Retriever’s efficiency is its bi-encoder structure. This setup allows for the independent encoding of freelancer and project information into high-dimensional vectors (called embeddings). The key advantage here is the pre-computability of these embeddings: Freelancer profiles can be encoded ahead of time and stored, ready to be compared against any new project that comes in. When a new project is posted, its details are encoded into a vector in real-time. This project vector is then compared against the precomputed freelancer vectors using a similarity measure, like cosine similarity.

In order to build these embeddings, we needed to develop two models (one for freelancer profiles and another one for projects). The details of these models, developed by our data science team would require a dedicated article so we won’t cover that here. Instead, the main facts about them:
- They are built on top of transformers: this architecture of machine learning models, proposed by Google in 2017 is state-of-the-art in the field of NLP (Natural Language Processing) and at the core of famous models like BERT or GPT. These models go beyond mere keyword matching, allowing the system to understand context, synonyms, and related concepts, providing a more nuanced and accurate matching. For instance, a project seeking a ‘Java developer’ will not only match freelancers with ‘Java’ listed in their skills, but can also consider those with related skills or experience, like ‘Spring boot’ or ‘J2EE,’ even if the exact word ‘Java’ is not present in their profile.
- Overall training objective of these encoders is to do a projection of freelancers and projects in a semantic space, where:
- freelancers with similar skills and experiences are close to each other
- projects with required skills are close to freelancers with this skills

Once the models were ready, we had to think about the infrastructure to store the precomputed vectors and serve them efficiently. For these purposes, a vector database seemed to be the perfect solution.
At the core of the infrastructure: a vector database
We quickly considered looking at vector database solutions in our architecture, as we identified three main benefits:
- Access to the best ANN algorithms: One of the core strengths of vector databases is their use of Approximate Nearest Neighbor (ANN) algorithms. These algorithms allow the system to quickly find the closest vectors in high-dimensional space without exhaustively comparing every point. This capability is crucial for the Retriever phase, where the system must rapidly identify a subset of freelancers whose profiles are semantically close to a given project’s requirements and vector databases offer great implementation of these algorithms.
- Maintaining a single, up-to-date, source of truth: By centralizing the vector representations of freelancers and projects in one place, vector databases serve as a single source of truth for our matching system. Also, as freelancer profiles could evolve quickly, vector databases offer upsert operations and in that way ensure that our matching recommendations are always based on the latest, most relevant information.
- Scalability and maintainability: As the Malt platform grows and evolves, so does the volume of demand on our matching system. Vector databases offer the scalability and flexibility required to accommodate this growth. They can efficiently handle increasing volumes of data and queries, ensuring that our system remains responsive and effective regardless of scale.
In summary, the adoption of a vector database was a crucial step in the development of our new matching system. In 2022, this technology became very popular (especially for Generative AI related applications) and the number of actors on the market exploded. In the next section, we explain how we approached the selection of the database and evaluated the various options.
Choosing the ideal database
Our selection of vector database technology was guided by a set of criteria, each reflecting a core need for the us:
- Performance: The speed of query processing is paramount. A faster database enhances user experience by delivering quick results, allows new usage (like real-time recommendation) and enables our system to scale effectively while supply and demand grow on Malt.
- Approximate Nearest Neighbors (ANN) Quality: The goal of our Retriever is to identify the closest vectors in the semantic space. High-quality ANN algorithms ensure that the database can retrieve the most relevant matches with precision, directly impacting the effectiveness of the matching system.
- Filtering Capabilities: Our matching process is not only about vector similarity but it also includes various business filters (like location, availability, etc.) defined by the Malt product team. The chosen database needed to support all these filtering without significant performance trade-offs.
Is filtering absolutely necessary to be performed within the vector database? What about applying it before or after the semantic search?
- If you apply the filtering before the semantic search, you will have to send to the vector database the list of all the filtered freelancers (basically a binary mask) to exclude them during search. This could lead to huge payloads and impact performance and scalability. Also, depending on the implementation used by the vector database for the ANN, it could degrade the quality of the search. In particular, a lot of vector databases are using the HSNW (Hierarchical Navigable Small Worlds) algorithm. This algorithm relies on graphs, where freelancers and projects are the nodes of the graph. Deactivating too much nodes could lead to a sparse, non navigable, graph and impact quality of the search
- If you apply the filtering after the semantic search, you will have to retrieve a lot of elements to be sure you have results after the filtering step. This will impact the performance of the retriever, as it is correlated to the number of candidates requested. It is especially true if the filters are “agressives” (meaning, they remove a lot of potential candidates).
We benchmarked several vector databases, testing their performance, ANN quality, and filtering capabilities under conditions that mimicked our real-world use case. Some of the databases we evaluated included:
- Elasticsearch: A database we already use and are familiar with at Malt. Initially developed as a full-text search engine, it offers dense vector search since version 8
- PGVector: An extension for the well-known PostgreSQL (a relational database that we also use at Malt)
- Qdrant: A dedicated vector database, fairly new to the market compared to the others (version 1.0 was released in February 2023). Qdrant shows impressive performance in their benchmarks and it got us pretty curious.
Why didn’t you benchmark more solutions / <insert your favorite> vector database than just these three?
In fact we took a look at other providers, including well-known players like Pinecone but we had to exclude them. That is mainly because of the filtering capabilities of the databases: our use case includes filtering on geo-spatial data (to recommend freelancers next to projects) and only few databases support this feature currently.

This benchmark revealed significant variations in performance and capabilities:
- While Elasticsearch provided a familiar environment with good filtering options, its performance in high-dimensional vector searches lagged behind Qdrant.
- PGVector presented an intriguing blend of relational and vector database features, but its overall performance and ANN quality did not meet our benchmarks for speed and precision.
- Qdrant stood out with a strong balance of high-speed retrieval, effective ANN implementations, and robust filtering capabilities, aligning well with our needs for scalability, precision, and flexibility.
After thorough evaluation, Qdrant emerged as the optimal choice for our system, offering the best blend of speed, accuracy, and functionality. Its ability to handle our specific demands for vector search and filtering (especially geo-spatial), combined with its scalability and performance, made it the clear winner in our selection process.
Deployment of Qdrant
The process of implementing and deploying our database of choice was crucial to ensure that the theoretical benefits of the vector database would translate into tangible improvements in the matching system’s performance and reliability.
Cluster configuration
To maximize the availability and scalability, Qdrant has the capability to be deployed in cluster configuration. This setup involved multiple Qdrant nodes organized in a manner that ensures data is replicated and balanced across the cluster, providing both redundancy and efficient query processing.
- Sharding: We divided our data into several shards, with each shard containing a portion of the dataset. This distribution allows for parallel processing, enhancing the speed of data retrieval and updates.
- Replication: Each shard was replicated across different nodes. This redundancy ensures that even if one node encounters an issue, the system remains operational, providing high availability.
Deployment
We decided to self-host the solution on our Kubernetes clusters. Qdrant team provides Docker image (encapsulating the database along with its dependencies, ensuring consistency across different development and production environments) and an Helm chart that contains the definition of all the needed Kubernetes objects, streamlining the deployment process, making it reproducible and scalable.
Regarding the monitoring, Qdrant exposes its metrics in Prometheus/OpenMetrics format and we coupled this with Grafana to provide real-time insights into the health, performance, and availability of our Qdrant nodes, allowing us to quickly identify and address any issues.
Outcomes and next steps
As a result of this work, we deployed our retriever-ranker architecture in 2023. We placed our new neural retriever, backed by the vector database, before the current matching model. This marked a significant milestone for our matching system. We saw a huge improvement in response times, with p95 latency dropping from tens of seconds (sometimes taking over a minute!) to a snappy 3 seconds at most. All this was achieved without sacrificing the quality of our recommendations, which was confirmed by an increase in the conversion of projects in our AB test.
This performance improvement goes beyond technical success. It unlocks new possibilities for Malt, enabling us to recommend freelancers in near real-time, significantly enhancing the user experience.
Our key takeaways
- Embedding Model: We confirmed the value of training our own model, specialized on our data, compared to using a generic embedding model (such as those from Sentence-Transformers).
- Vector Database Landscape: This technology is still young and evolving. Each vendor has made significant improvements since our last benchmark, and we remain open to adopting new solutions as they emerge (our code is designed for quick swaps between vendors).
- Filtering: This feature is still underrated in vector databases, yet it can be crucial for certain applications.
- Semantic Search: While very powerful, there are scenarios where traditional keyword searches prove more effective. Consequently, vector databases are beginning to support hybrid searches that combine semantic and keyword searches. We foresee this becoming an essential feature in the future, with Elasticsearch particularly well positioned to excel, given their extensive experience in traditional search.
What’s next for us?
The validation of our embedding model and retriever-ranker architecture opens doors for continued exploration and future developments:
- Expanding the retriever applications: We envision broader use-cases for our retriever, such as recommending profiles based on a given profile (first projecting a profile into the latent space, then using an ANN to identify the closest matches).
- Next-gen ranker: We’re currently developing a state-of-the-art ranker that should further meaningfully improve the quality of matching.
