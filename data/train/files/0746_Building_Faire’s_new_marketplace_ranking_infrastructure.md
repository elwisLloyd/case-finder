Building Faire’s new marketplace ranking infrastructure
Faire is a wholesale marketplace that connects over 250,000 retailers with the world’s best independent brands. Small business owners across North America and Europe use Faire to discover the best products to sell to their customers.
Traditionally, these retailers have stocked their stores by attending in-person trade shows. Once or twice a year, they would attend a trade show and place purchase orders for 6–12 months to stock their store. Purchasing decisions were made by a combination of experience and word of mouth, with the objective of meeting consumer demand. If the inventory didn’t sell well, the retailers had to mark down the items or keep the items sitting on their shelves.
Faire’s marketplace provides a new and innovative platform for retailers to find the perfect inventory. We offer three core discovery surfaces to make it seamless for retailers to find great products in whatever way is most comfortable for them. Category navigation surfaces products by category according to our product taxonomy — think ‘Home Decor’, ‘Bedding’, ‘Bath & Body’, etc. Search uses text-matching to enable retailers to drill down and find specific products to meet their needs. Finally, the brand page showcases products from a single brand, allowing retailers to meet wholesale order minimums and foster new stockist relationships.
At Faire, all three of these core surfaces are powered by one unified ranking infrastructure that we refer to internally as Real-Time Ranking. This post will describe the design and engineering journey, from the time we launched as a company and implemented the simplest ranking system possible, to our latest real-time ranking infra.
The Before Times
While a lot has changed at Faire over the years, one constant has been our high-level take on the challenge of surfacing the right products in the right context. All of our systems have nominally been composed of two steps: retrieval and ranking.
Retrieval is the process of finding a candidate set of products that may be of interest to a retailer in a particular context. For example, when a retailer searches for the word “candle” we may retrieve 500–1000 products that contain the word “candle” in their title, description, or metadata. Or when a retailer navigates to “Home Decor”, we may retrieve a few hundred products that are tagged with the “Home Decor” category.
Ranking acts during or after retrieval in order to optimize the ordering of the products that we return. This is typically done using a numeric score that represents a marketplace or business objective. When we launched Faire, we didn’t have the time or necessary resources to build sophisticated ranking systems, and instead, used very simple retrieval and re-ranking systems that helped us get off the ground. Our very first retrieval system, built in 2017, was based on simple MySQL queries running against our live database, which was obviously limited in capability and slow. When this system started to break, we updated each of our core surfaces in a piecemeal fashion. In search, we moved to a simple reverse index managed by an outside company. We later migrated to ElasticSearch (more on that below). In the meantime, we still had MySQL-based retrieval for the brand page and category navigation. This created myriad inconsistencies in both our backend architecture and the user experience across surfaces: user-selectable filters were different between search and category navigation, text-matching rules were totally different between product search and brand-page search, and our impressions logging followed different schemas for each surface. These headaches taught us that retrieval should be unified across surfaces. Over time, we worked to resolve these differences, and as we better understood our goals and requirements, we built a single retrieval mechanism based on Elasticsearch.
Originally, ranking stopped at retrieval. We retrieved a list of potentially matching products and then returned that same exact list to the retailer in the same order as it was retrieved. Eventually, we realized that we needed a way to re-rank the list to optimize for conversion and improve the relevance of the products shown. Our first reranking system was entirely based on offline scores. Each product and brand had a score that was computed offline by a set of queries/models in our data warehouse. We uploaded these scores nightly to our servers, where they were used to re-rank the retrieved results to generate the final list displayed to retailers.
While this hodgepodge of systems was sufficient to get us off the ground, it was clear that it would not allow us to provide the quality of results that best served our retailers. Computing model scores offline was very limited in its ability to offer precise predictions by retailer or by search query. Making such granular predictions required computing pairwise scores for all retailer-product or query-product pairs. This scaled very poorly, taking longer and longer to compute all the scores for all the products, queries, and retailers in the system, due to the cartesian explosion of all their possible combinations.
Faced with the challenge of providing high-quality, granular, and personalized ranking scores, two years ago, we embarked on a project to build a platform that would consolidate our existing retrieval and ranking systems, while enabling us to scale rapidly for years to come.
Requirements
When we set out to build our new system, we agreed on a set of requirements we wanted to meet:
Latency. A real-time ranking system comes with many tight constraints, the most important being latency. We knew from previous research that each millisecond counts when returning results. Search latencies of up to 500ms tend to be not noticeable by users [1], so we chose that as our server side request/response latency budget.
Extensibility. We wanted our new real-time ranking system to be generic enough to be applicable to all search and recommendation surfaces at Faire. This meant that we needed to be able to support multiple item types. More specifically, on some surfaces we display products, while on other surfaces we display brands or carousels as individual entities. By supporting multiple item types, we knew we would be able to reuse the same infrastructure to rank any of these entities across all surfaces.
Developer Experience. Another key requirement was the ease with which the new system could be adopted. We broke this down into two separate sub-requirements:
- Easy to plug into an existing surface. We wanted to minimize the upfront cost required to port a legacy surface to the new system.
- Easy to extend with new machine learning features and models. We wanted the system to remove as many barriers as possible to adding new ML features, allowing data scientists and ML engineers alike to iterate quickly.
Data Fidelity. We wanted to guarantee that the data used in offline training was computed the exact same way as the data used in production by the model.
Monitoring and Alerting. Any complex system is subject to a large number of failure modes. Real-time ranking is no exception to that rule. This meant that we needed to be able to monitor system health and build monitoring and alerting from the beginning.
System components
Once we clearly identified our requirements, we needed to design the components of the system and assemble them to achieve our goals.
This is a high-level overview of the system. Below, we will describe each component, as well as the design and implementation considerations for each.
Retrieval
There are currently several million active products on Faire. This is far too large of a candidate pool to rank on each request — we need to work with a smaller list. This requires a search index that is able to quickly narrow down the candidate pool in response to a query. For our retrieval, we chose ElasticSearch because of its broad functionality, scalability, and ease of deployment.

For any given request, we fetch ~1000 of the most relevant candidates from ElasticSearch to re-rank. For search, ElasticSearch determines relevance based on a weighted scoring function using BM25 (a fancy version of TF-IDF) at its core. In category navigation, we reformulate our query to fetch the top products by quality score in the specified category. Finally, for the brand page, we simply query all products in the specified brand’s catalog.
We are also working on adding embedding-based retrieval to our ElasticSearch cluster, in order to support recommendation surfaces that don’t follow a heuristic query rule. We currently power brand and product similarity using ML-based embeddings, that either power offline-generated top-k nearest neighbors (kNN) datasets, or are otherwise indexed for approximate-kNN retrieval using a parallel in-memory system. Serving these embeddings with ElasticSearch will enable filtered retrieval of recommended products (e.g. ‘Similar products from brands in your state’), semantic search, and combinations of relevance and personalization.
Once we have 1000 products to re-rank, we proceed through the rest of the real-time system.
Feature store
For each one of the products in the candidate set, we need to evaluate a model on the fly. This means that when a request comes in, we need to retrieve and/or compute machine learning features based on the retailer making the request, the request context (whether it’s search, category navigation, etc.), and the candidate products. To maximize the flexibility and performance of our feature set, we implemented a system that uses both offline batch-computed and online real-time computed features.
Offline Feature Computation
We run offline computation of two types of data for ingestion by our real-time ranking system: features and embeddings. Features are essentially single numeric data points representing trends, facts, and aggregate statistics about products, brands, and retailers. Meanwhile, embeddings are vectors that represent more complex characteristics, such as a retailer’s order history, a brand’s product catalog, or the semantic meaning of a text snippet.
One powerful motivation for computing embeddings is as a solution to the space complexity of computing pairwise interaction features. For instance, in search ranking, we might want to measure the text similarity between a search query and a product name. We could naively compute a similarity feature offline but would need to do so for all possible combinations of 100k+ queries and 1M+ products. Alternatively, we could simply compute embeddings for each query and product individually–in real-time, we can then fetch each query and product embedding and compute their similarity.
Both features and embeddings are computed offline using a combination of SQL queries and ML models using our data warehouse and orchestrated via Airflow. At the time of writing, we have over 2000 features and several dozen embeddings computed on a regular cadence.
Online Feature Computation
Offline features and embeddings are computed in our data warehouse on a daily basis. To serve these features online, we pivot into a key:value schema that facilitates easy retrieval of all features and embeddings for a single entity (retailer, brand, product, etc). This map is stored on a designated Redis cluster to ensure low latency during ranking.
Other features can only be computed in real-time, using a combination of offline and online data. We compute online features using our main backend service, providing easy access to a wide range of data sources. For example, when a retailer signs up on Faire and completes our onboarding style questionnaire, we might compute corresponding online features by querying their responses from our production database. We also take advantage of our offline feature store to generate rich interaction features by fetching precomputed embeddings for each entity and computing similarity metrics on the fly. Finally, we cache recent retailer activity and use it to provide real-time personalization via similarities to recently viewed products, recent search queries, and more.
The design of this feature pipeline was very important to us. We wanted it to be both powerful enough to allow us to express complex features, and easy enough to enable all data scientists and ML engineers to contribute. In the end, we settled for a pattern that enables creating features and including them in a new ranking context with few lines of code. Offline Learning
Our re-ranking models are typically XGBoost models trained on add-to-carts using a pointwise loss function. From previous experience, we know that one of the most time-consuming and error-prone aspects of our model lifecycle is ensuring data fidelity between training and inference time. We addressed this problem using a ‘log and wait’ approach. Essentially, all retrieved and computed features for every item shown to a retailer are logged and streamed into our data warehouse. Downstream ETL pipelines automatically pivot these logged features and combine them with training labels. We train models using these logged features directly, ensuring that feature computation is 100% consistent.
This approach, while maximizing data fidelity, is not without drawbacks. First, some lead time is required between designing a new feature and incorporating it into a trained ranking model. In practice, there are generally a few weeks between launching a new feature and logging enough training data for our new ranking model to pick it up. Second, we use considerable space in our data warehouse logging features which may turn out to not be useful for ranking. Despite these drawbacks, our approach has so far enabled rapid iterations on our ranking models by minimizing the toil associated with wrangling training data.
Model Store
Models are retrained periodically and uploaded to an AWS S3 bucket with all associated model artifacts. The system is tightly integrated into our internal experimentation framework so that launching a new model only involves uploading the model artifact to S3 and configuring a setting to point to the new model object, therefore requiring minimal or no code at all to deploy new models. Since models are retrained using only logged features, feature coverage is near-guaranteed, minimizing any potential pitfalls or launch-day issues typically associated with synchronizing new models and their feature dependencies.
Model Evaluation in Re-ranking
Once all the pieces of the puzzles above are in place, it’s time to score each product using one of our pre-trained models. As mentioned above we currently rely heavily on XGBoost models although we will be working on supporting neural network models in the near future. The reason we chose XGBoost models for our current version is inference speed, tooling maturity, and experience in the team. Additional benefits include good null handling, as well as tolerance for a wide range of numeric feature distributions with minimal preprocessing or normalization required.
We re-rank all products in our candidate set using the scores obtained by our ranking model. We then pass this list to a secondary ranker that works on solving secondary marketplace objectives–stay tuned for more on that in an upcoming post.
Conclusion
In this post, we’ve outlined the requirements of real-time ranking at Faire and described our journey to building an in-house, end-to-end system to enable rapid training of ranking models with rich feature sets and minimal production headaches. Since we launched it, this system has served hundreds of millions of requests and has helped us greatly improve conversion across the marketplace, ultimately creating a better experience for our customers. We continue to find opportunities to improve it, in order to support more functionality and help the system scale.
