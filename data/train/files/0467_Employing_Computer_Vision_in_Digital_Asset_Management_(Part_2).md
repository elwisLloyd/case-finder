Employing Computer Vision in Digital Asset Management — Part II
Performing AI optical character recognition and analysis at scale.
Introduction
Computer Vision is being used by the Data Science Team in partnership with Marketing Technology (MarTech) at Haleon to streamline our digital asset management systems. Digital asset management is the technical process, and associated software, for managing the organisation, storage, and reuse of digital files and media assets. The details of this work at Haleon were described in a previous article. Here we describe the technical aspects of this initiative. This encompasses the efforts to apply Artificial Intelligence and Optical Character Recognition at scale, by building a DevOps pipeline that can distribute the parallel processes, and then sample the results to ensure they are robust and reliable.
Optical Character Recognition
In order to glean information from product imagery, experiments were conducted using a form of AI called optical character recognition (OCR). OCR is a software that recognises, extracts, and parses characters and words from image data. It is used in various vision systems for scanning documents, recognising numbers, and providing assistive technology for the visually impaired (for example the Seeing AI app). It proved highly effective at reading information such as product branding and descriptions from digital assets, which can populate otherwise missing metadata for these assets, improving their sortation and searchability for reuse and adaptation.
Initial experiments using open-source OCR packages, such as PyTesseract and EasyOCR, demonstrated the effectiveness of extracting information from samples of digital assets. This proved the concept and motivated the design and build of a system that could apply OCR at scale. Haleon partners with Microsoft Azure for its cloud computing infrastructure and Azure Cognitive Services provides access to a suite of computer vision software including an OCR service that is accessible via API.
While OCR sits at the core of the process, a number of component steps were integrated into the project pipeline: a preprocessing stage to handle the variety of file types including movie files; AI keyword generation; fuzzy-matching against the product taxonomy; and a statistical sampling method for assessing the accuracy and reliability of the results. Solution Architecture
A number of considerations were made to design and build a pipeline that could perform the steps outlined above for analysing millions of assets. There was a need to ensure it was both scalable and future-proof, so that additional elements of the pipeline could be added or current elements adapted/replaced. It also needed to handle large amounts of data in various formats, especially the many folders of large movie files across global campaigns. The potential was recognised for extending to other services such as the Azure Video Indexer to unlock further capabilities for future scene description, speech-to-text, and facial detection which is useful when campaigns include talent hires such as actors.
The resulting components are a demonstration of the cutting-edge technology behind the computer vision pipeline enabled by Haleon’s DevOps infrastructure and Azure cloud computing to deliver scalable AI techniques. Interframe Similarity
When decomposing movie files into their component frames, many of those frames will appear similar to each other — referred to as interframe similarity. For example, unless the scene in a movie changes, many of the frames will show the same information. This type of frame analysis is used in data transfer such as video transcoding to save on bandwidth. A metric for measuring the difference between frames enables a sampling process whereby only the frames that differ in a statistically significant way are analysed for their information, reducing cost. By first downsampling the movies to a justifiably useful frame rate, a mathematically simple “image differencing” approach can demonstrate the effectiveness of comparing frames across the samples without adding unnecessary complexity to the pipeline process. This is illustrated below for two example frames in a movie asset for a Voltaren campaign. The summary statistics of the image differencing builds up two populations that can be identified and lead to meaningful separation thresholds to apply to the frames. The instances where a significant information gain is observed reveal the frames worth keeping for analysis. This is an efficiency gain when dealing with large amounts of data, realised through the benefit of reduced compute time and associated compute costs incurred in analysing the image contents using AI.
Storyboards
Once frames are sampled from the movie assets, summary “storyboards” can be built in a way that consolidates as much information as possible into a single image file, thus reducing the overhead of the resulting image analysis. This is motivated by the realised cost reduction of putting a storyboard through the computer vision pipeline instead of the many separate frames, which otherwise accumulate costs when scaled over millions of assets. The current API limits are a maximum file size of 4MB and maximum image dimensions of 16,000 by 16,000 pixels. Given these limits, a storyboard of frames is built by filling out the available pixel limits with the maximum number of frames in the horizontal and vertical image dimensions. Reducing entire movie files to a small number of storyboards results in a cost reduction of approximately 80% across the many video assets analysed in the computer vision pipeline. Fuzzy Logic
When OCR reads text there can be misspellings due to errors in interpreting the characters and words presented to it. Limitations exist regarding the size of the words in the imagery, and whereabouts the characters appear on the image relative to each other. OCR can also face some confusion around the graphic design in certain branding. A fuzzy matching logic on the resulting text provides a workaround for minimising these errors when attempting to identify brands belonging to Haleon’s product taxonomy. A processing step for this can include techniques from the field of Natural Language Processing (NLP) to best prepare the text for analysing it with fuzzy logic. This can include tokenisation to split the words according to specific rules, or stemming to reduce words to their root. Similar processing is done to the file paths of the assets for extracting any useful information that can complement the metadata, such as when assets sit in a folder that is named according to the campaign for example.

Text matching algorithms work by assigning similarity scores to different strings. An example of a brand that OCR can find challenging is “Emergen-C” which sometimes comes out as just “Emergen”. A Levenshtein distance metric between these strings is determined by the amount of insertions, deletions, or substitutions needed to map from one to the other. So “Emergen” is 2 insertions away from “Emergen-C”, as illustrated below. Thresholds are set from inspections of samples of data and their OCR results to tailor it for the best accuracy in this context.
EXIF Information Extraction
Exchangeable image file format (Exif) is a standard for holding embedded metadata in media files, notably digital images that have information on where, when, and how the image was taken. In some instances this can reveal the age of an asset, through a Creation Date or variation of it. Older assets can be moved to cold storage in an effort to reduce costs if these are less likely to be reused in future campaigns or market adaptations. There is a computational expense in mining Exif information from assets stored as blobs in an Azure container, so some rules can be put in place to maximise the usefulness of this regarding the file type and file size of the asset. For example, jpeg, tiff, png, mov and mp4 file types were found to most often hold Exif with a Creation Date although some checks are needed against edge cases showing erroneous years or different datetime formats.
Sample Proportion Analysis
When analysing millions of assets by putting them through the computer vision pipeline there is a challenge to understand how reliable the results are without being able to validate every asset individually because of the scale of the dataset. To overcome this, a sampling methodology was designed and utilised for checking the level of coverage and accuracy of the resulting tags on the assets — where coverage is how many assets were tagged, and accuracy is how many times the tag is correct. This is treated as a population proportion problem according to the equation P=X/N where X is the count of successes in a population of size N.
To do this, a sample of assets may be selected and treated as a representative sample of the overall population of assets if it meets certain criteria outlined below.
- Given a total population size N_total representing the number of asset files, there will be N_tagged assets contained in those N_total assets that already hold metadata, where N_tagged < N_total due to the known level of sparsity in the metadata.
- A baseline is established by performing a manual inspection of a sample of assets n that are expected to have n_tags and this is treated as the initial coverage and accuracy measure. The underlying assumption here is that the pre-populated metadata of the N_tagged should be 100% accurate so we challenge this assumption by inspecting it for such limits as human error.
- A sample of the N_tagged assets that were pre-populated with the target metadata and have been analysed by the computer vision pipeline is selected as n_sample. The match between the tags is checked to determine how well it has performed in terms of coverage and accuracy.
- A form of bootstrapping is undertaken by re-sampling n_sample from N_tagged with replacement x times to repeat the inspection of the coverage and accuracy of the tags. This bootstrapping will result in a distribution that can be assumed to exhibit normality meaning the sample statistics should converge on the population statistics according to the Central Limit Theorem. Thus, we have an estimate of the coverage and accuracy being achieved across the total population N_total.
Other useful patterns can emerge from this sampling process to indicate which assets are performing better or worse than others and focus development efforts on improving those as needed.
Conclusion
The successful scale-up of computer vision techniques has proven very valuable for mining useful metadata from digital assets at Haleon in support of their sortation, storage, and searchability. This is enabled through the purpose-built cloud pipeline that employs a series of scientific techniques for processing and analysing digital assets using AI and OCR. The size of the data, at over a petabyte of media files, is prohibitively large for undertaking manual inspections to achieve this, so the use of cloud compute facilities has been key to the successful delivery of this AI solution.
