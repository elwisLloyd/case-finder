How we used machine learning to fight fraud at BlaBlaCar — Part 1
In this series of articles, we will share BlaBlaCar’s best practices in managing a strategic project involving machine learning. These articles do require basic knowledge in Machine Learning. Our approach is very much inspired by Google and their Google Machine Learning rule book.

In this first article, we will share the considerations and tools set up before implementing machine learning systems. This article is aimed at engineers or managers who want to increase the chances of success of a project involving machine learning algorithms.
Before Machine Learning
What is Phishing?
Phishing is a type of fraud where scammers attempt to obtain payment or financial details from individuals by posing as a legitimate entity. Platforms that facilitate contacts between individuals and that involve some sort of money exchange between them, such as BlaBlaCar, are particularly vulnerable to this type of fraud scheme.
To combat this, BlaBlaCar has implemented various fraud detection and fraud management systems, some of which include machine learning algorithms. This series of articles will focus on one of them for which a machine learning oriented approach generated high payoff.
How do scammers try to get money or payment details?
Scammers often use tactics such as building fake websites that resemble the legitimate platform or creating fake user profiles. These fake users may have no ratings and charge less than others for a similar ride. They may attempt to contact members outside of the platform to obtain payment details, an obvious breach of security.
Social Engineering
In the context of information security, social engineering refers to the manipulation of individuals to divulge sensitive information or perform actions that may compromise their security. Cognitive biases are often exploited by social engineers to achieve their objectives, and these techniques are widely used by scammers on platforms like BlaBlaCar. In particular, scammers tend to rely on principles of influence such as scarcity and urgency to elicit a response from their targets.
For instance, imagine a user who is eagerly waiting for a carpool to join their family for the weekend. If the trip is unexpectedly canceled, the user may experience a sense of urgency and distress, prompting them to take actions they would not normally consider. As such, it is crucial to protect BlaBlaCar members from these types of situations.
At BlaBlaCar, we are committed to implementing measures to safeguard our users against social engineering attacks. By educating our members about the risks and providing guidance on how to identify and respond to potential threats, we strive to create a secure environment for everyone on our platform. On top of member awareness campaigns, our services need to have security features built in, so as to make it harder to use them against our users.
Defining the “what” before the “how”
But what does it mean to secure our services against scammers? We know we have a potential issue, and it may be tempting to rush head-first into small fixes, trying to prevent fake accounts from being created.
Our strategy, however, was to take a step back. What is it, precisely, that we’re trying to solve? It is crucial to first define a metric that needs to be improved to address the problem at hand. Without measuring the problem, it becomes difficult to optimize or find a solution, even with machine learning algorithms. So don’t rush trying to find solutions to problems that are hard to define or measure. Without clarifying them first, chances are the solutions won’t be the right ones.
First, design and implement metrics
Definition: Key performance indicators (KPIs) are strategic while metrics are often operational or tactical. Metrics are lower-level indicators specific to a department while KPIs can be tracked by various departments working towards the same goal.
In the fight against fraud, we picked a KPI that is simple, observable and accurate. We decided to measure the number of scam cases reported by our members via our contact form. We expressed it as the number of victims per thousand active members. Each quarter, country based objective and alert levels are defined for this indicator and shared across the company.
However, relying solely on the number of fraud cases reported by members is not ideal to track trends on a weekly basis as it has the tendency of being late, as reports of fraud cases can take up to 3 weeks to be communicated by the victim. If reducing the volume of fraud victims to below 1 per 10k active members is a great company-wide goal, we had to build proxy metrics for the daily operations of each team. A proxy metric is a metric that is easy to measure and correlated with our company-wide goal. When deciding upon the proxy metrics to be measured, we aimed for more than 1 metric, and tried to avoid those that are measuring indirect effects. Each proxy metric is bringing a unique perspective and biases, so we took some time to identify and list them.

Choosing the right metric is critical, as it will dictate the actions that can be taken to address the issue. For instance, in the initial stages of fighting fraud at BlaBlaCar, we focused on reducing the percentage of bookings made on trips published by scammers. However, as fraud was brought under control, we moved our focus on the percentage of fraudulent supply. In both cases these metrics were computed each day by manually labeling a sample of the active drivers.
Do you see the change in perspective between both metrics? The percentage of booking requests made to scammers reflects the behavior of both our legitimate members and the scammers, whereas the percentage of fraudulent supply mostly captures the actions of scammers. This meant that our member awareness campaigns had an impact on the first metric but not on the second.
Whether we wanted to focus on one or the other metrics was a matter of product prioritization. Member education was a cheap and effective way to address the initial metric but negatively impacted the second one as increasing the awareness of our members forced scammers to change their strategy and they ended up publishing even more fraudulent trips. Therefore, it was important to continually evaluate both metrics and adjust our strategy accordingly.
Make it work. Then, make it scalable.
We knew that in order to successfully implement a machine learning system, it was important to first make sure that the problem was worth the investment of resources, both human and financial. In many cases, starting with simple tools and manual actions can have a significant impact on the key performance indicator without the need for a complex machine learning system.
Machine learning systems are impressive but they usually need some degree of human oversight. Having experts on hand to assist data scientists is an invaluable shortcut and can often make all the difference. If a team is unable to dedicate substantial human resources to use straightforward tools and frequently rely on manual actions to influence key performance indicators, then the issue is likely not significant enough to warrant attention.
In the case of fraud at BlaBlaCar, the team first focused on manually reviewing user cohorts where the proportion of fraud was highest and where the company’s strategic interest could be threatened. This allowed us to collect data and labels while also putting out the immediate fire of scammers posting fake trips. Once the issue was under control, the team was able to focus on building a more scalable and automated solution, which could be used to monitor and detect fraudulent behavior on a larger scale.
We invest significant human resources on manual labeling of our members.
The lesson here is that it is important to prioritize and focus on solving the most pressing issues first, before investing in complex and expensive machine learning systems. By starting small and iterating based on the results, we were able to build a more effective solution over time, while decreasing the pressure on the team. For most companies, a machine learning system represents a significant investment over several years. So pick your fights with care.
Choose machine learning over a complex heuristic
Most anti-fraud systems are based on rule engines and ours is no different. Just spend a few minutes with a Trust & Safety expert, who is out there everyday blocking scammers, and you’ll come back with a long list of more or less specific business rules that help define suspicious activity on our platform. Each of those rules will catch scammers but it will also generate false positives. Let’s take a realistic, albeit made-up, example: we have noticed that a significant part of the trips published on the hour (6:00, 7:00, …) are the result of scripts periodically executed by fraudsters. After setting up a rule to detect these trips, the fraudsters changed the schedule of their scripts and we now essentially capture trips published by legit users who by chance published their trips on the dot.
When choosing between a complex heuristic and a machine learning system, we had to consider the limitations of both solutions. While rule engines may catch scammers, they also generate false positives that can lead to frustration for legitimate users. Additionally, scammers may quickly adapt and bypass rules, rendering them useless over time.
While the volume of legitimate users impacted by a business rule may seem small compared to the number of scammers that we detected, the situation can quickly turn around as scammers change their behavior. It is estimated that it takes less than a week for a new rule to be bypassed by scammers.
Machine learning systems can adapt to changing behavior and improve over time, if they are retrained frequently. They also have the potential to reduce the number of false positives and the shifting speed by learning from past examples and identifying more nuanced patterns of fraud. But we noticed that without proper tooling the retraining frequency was quite low.
In order to avoid getting stuck with a set of business or machine learning rules that generate almost only false positives, we decided to first put in place the tools to evaluate the actual performance of these rules. These tools would be useful regardless of whether we picked a heuristic or a machine learning system.
The rise and fall of two business rules detection
We considered the business rules as the initial version of our machine learning model, the result of the condensed experience of a human expert “trained” on thousands of scam examples. As any machine learning model, business rules performance decreased as the behavior of our scammers shifted.
Conclusion
In conclusion, before training our first model, we identified a problem already being addressed by our company’s teams, pinpointed an actionable metric to track progress, and built a dashboard to give visibility to everyone involved. We’ve interviewed domain experts and deployed a simple heuristic based on a few rules to address part of the problem.
Most importantly, we’ve invested significant time in building a tool to evaluate the performance of each rule in our initial release. It was crucial to share how the measurement tool works and ensure all stakeholders had confidence in the results and dashboards available to them. Without this confidence, it would have been challenging to convince sponsors of the effectiveness of machine learning models, which are less explainable than simple business rules.
