# Item2Vec: Neural Item Embeddings to enhance recommendations

Источник: https://tech.olx.com/item2vec-neural-item-embeddings-to-enhance-recommendations-1fd948a6f293

Item recommendation is a huge part of modern e-commerce helping you find products you might be interested in. Content-based matching and collaborative filtering are among well-recognized and widely used methods to generate such recommendations. There is also a relatively new neural-network-based approach called Item2Vec. Taking inspiration from Natural Language Processing it maps items into a common “semantic” high-dimensional space where those can be compared using the associated vectors. In this post, we’ll do a quick comparison of the different algorithms and share our experience adopting Item2Vec to improve the quality of recommendations at OLX Europe (online classifieds). In particular, we’ll cover:
- How to recommend items to items
- How to turn items into vectors using neural networks
- What Item2Vec brings to the table
Business context
OLX is an online marketplace where sellers post their ads describing the items they’re selling, and buyers try to find the best deal. Navigating through an online marketplace you can face a few types of recommendations different in intent:
- Item-to-Item recommendation aims to provide the most similar items to a given one, you can normally see these recommendations on an item page in the “similar to this” section.
- User-to-Item recommendation tries to guess your interests given your profile and, most importantly, your browsing history. These recommendations are typically shown on the home page.
Item-to-Item recommendations
In this article we’ll cover Item-to-Item recommender systems, Item2Vec being one of those. However, the User-to-Item recommendation problem can be easily cast to Item-to-Item, by considering the user history of interactions as a list of items the user viewed and generating recommendations to these items. So how do we do the Item-to-Item recommendations? There are two main distinct approaches we’ve been using to solve this problem.
The first and the main one is Collaborative Filtering (CF) which is purely interaction-based (or behavioural). Being content-agnostic it treats items as ID-s and compares them using only their co-occurrence statistics. In its simplest form CF treats an item A as a one-hot encoded vector of similarity to other items: 1 — if A is similar to B as if they were viewed by the same user in a single session, and 0 if not. An example of such an algorithm would be so-called ALS — you can read more about it in one of our previous blogposts. It’s a quite powerful approach as it models real user behavior and preferences. Being fairly impactful this algorithm still suffers from two interconnected issues.
The first issue is items don’t always get many interactions so the item-to-item matrix is very sparse and it’s not always possible to find meaningful recommendations — this is the data sparsity issue. The other issue is an extreme case of the sparsity problem — the “cold start”. The “cold start” means an item is just being posted and it has no associated interactions yet, which makes the item “cold”. In this case, we don’t know anything about the item in our paradigm. So we’re unable to recommend anything to it, and moreover, we can’t show this item on any other item’s page.
The second approach to item-to-item recommendations — content-based — overcomes the drawbacks of CF not requiring any interactions. For a given item it analyzes only the information provided by the seller and compares it to the information about other items to find the most similar ones. And as you can imagine items’ content varies a lot, for the same products different sellers obviously make different photos, people use different words, they don’t always use dedicated fields for certain properties and sometimes they even use different categories for the same products. This makes the comparison of items a very convoluted task. Another problem with this method is it’s not obvious how to weigh different qualities of items, as let’s say what’s more important: a similar price or a similar title? We don’t know until we extract these preferences from behavioural data — users’ interactions with the items. So this approach isn’t perfect either, however, it serves well as a fallback when collaborative filtering fails.
Considering these two approaches with their strengths and weaknesses, can’t we take the best of both worlds using some sort of a hybrid model that utilizes interactions being content-aware at the same time? In fact, there are a few ways to do it, and one of them is what we’ll be talking about in detail — Item2Vec.
Item2Vec
A few years ago Yahoo proposed a new recommender system — Prod2Vec — a synergy of Collaborative Filtering and Word2Vec — a Neural Network-based algorithm from Natural Language Processing. Only a bit later Microsoft independently gave it a name that resonates slightly better with online marketplaces — Item2Vec. It demonstrated great potential, but it didn’t solve the problem of “cold start” in particular. The issue was that an item was still represented by its ID as in vanilla CF. Unlike a natural language token (a letter or a word) an item carries a lot of information on its own — the content. To overcome this problem the approach was refined by Avito who were inspired by neural network recommenders.
Now let’s look at the whole algorithm closer.
First, each item is represented by a dense high-dimensional vector (an embedding) pretty much as a word in Word2Vec. When we want to compare 2 items we simply compare their respective vectors computing some similarity metric such as dot product or cosine similarity. The choice of the metric is determined by the training method in place, we’ll cover that shortly. Unlike word vectors, item embeddings are not simply assigned and learned directly but inferred given the items’ content. That allows the recommender system to be applied to any new item, thus solving the “cold start” problem.

The model that embeds an item into a vector space is called an encoder. Below you can see a schematic representation of such a model proposed by Avito and used currently in OLX:
The goal of the encoder is to embed/encode items in a way that respects some sort of similarity between them. That’s achieved during the training procedure encapsulating an important assumption. Similarity assumption is the core idea of the skip-gram model (Word2Vec). It states that items viewed by a user consequently in a single session are more similar than a random pair of items. In the picture below you can see 2 sessions generated by some users, maybe even the same one, but separated in time. Given this piece of data, we’ll try to optimize our model to predict a high level of closeness for the consequent pairs of items, and low for the others.
This is how we implement it:
- Positive pairs are generated the way described above
- Negative pairs are sampled randomly from the items pool
- The items are embedded using an encoder
- The similarity of the pairs is calculated as a dot product of the respective embeddings
- These similarities are normalized and placed against the desired values — 1 for positive and 0 for negative pairs
- Cross-entropy serves as a loss function to estimate the distance between the prediction (similarities) and the ground truth (ones and zeros)
- The loss function is finally minimized during the training by adjusting the encoder weights

Summing all this up, we use item content and user sessions to train a recommender system (namely encoder) that can be applied to any item on the platform (including fresh ones) and estimate how likely it’s to be viewed in the same session with another one. Picking up the most similar items is the last step left to generate recommendations for a given item.
Integration
The integration process wasn’t particularly smooth compared to simpler models. The technical challenges are left for another post as it is a big topic by itself. Here let us share some numbers on the business effect of Item2Vec that we evaluated via A/B-testing. It took us a few iterations to adjust the algorithm and pipelines to make it work and see a significant uplift in business metrics.
The main baseline we tested Item2Vec against was Item-item collaborative filtering that has been working pretty well in many scenarios. We started with Item page recommendations where CF was accompanied by a rule-based recommender to back it up in case of a “cold start”, which is the moment when Item2Vec shines. The effect of Item2Vec integration was estimated at 10–20% uplift in our target metrics — clicks and contacts from the affected recommendations section — as in how well we guess and satisfy user interests. The uplift strongly depends on the market and category of items as those differ in catalog size, user base, and amount of available data to build a model on.
Following the success in Item Page, we incorporated the new algorithm into Home Feed, recommending to users items similar to what they’ve interacted with before. There we estimated a similar contribution of Item2Vec as on Item Page. Interestingly the best performing configuration of recommender systems we’ve found is a combination of CF and Item2Vec, this ensemble performs significantly better than any of the individual recommenders in isolation, confirming the idea that recommendations benefit from diversity.
All in all the integration of Item2Vec into our platform led to a shift in the distribution of interactions made via search vs the ones made in the affected pages, users don’t navigate through the catalog as much and find what they want way faster now.
On top of the use cases we’ve covered, the item embeddings produced by the model have already been found to be useful in other applications at OLX such as monetization and ranking. We might talk about it another time, so don’t miss out!
Conclusion
That’s a long story short of how Item2Vec helped us to improve the user experience on OLX. To sum up, the approach enables you to
- Solve the “cold start” problem
- Delegate feature selection to a neural network
- Benefit from diversity in recommendation
