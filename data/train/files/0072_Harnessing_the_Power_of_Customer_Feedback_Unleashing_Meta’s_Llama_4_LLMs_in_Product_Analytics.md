Harnessing the Power of Customer Feedback: Unleashing Meta’s Llama 4 LLMs in Product Analytics
Authors: Tim Dorn, Tony Fu, Gaurav Shrivastava, Prasad Bhandarkar
In every complaint, a chance to improve. In every compliment, a clue to delight. When customers share thoughts about our products, we gain an opportunity to understand what matters most to them. Every day, users all over the world share feedback through product reviews, bug reports, survey responses, social media posts, and more — a treasure trove of internal and public data that helps us identify our customers’ preferences, pain points, and frustrations.
But there’s a catch: despite its potential, customer feedback data is often underutilized in analytics applications because it’s notoriously difficult to work with. The raw data is often hindered by noise, bias, and a lack of structure which can make it difficult to extract meaningful insights from.
Noise stems from anecdotal, inconsistent, or inauthentic feedback, which can dilute the value of genuine feedback.
- Real Example (inconsistent): “I need a refund because it doesn’t work… Sorry for the confusion. 5 stars though because it looks amazing! Great work!”
Bias occurs when relying on limited feedback sources. For example, focusing solely on bug reports can create a skewed perspective, emphasizing negative technical issues.
- Real Example (trolling): “Most people leaving low reviews are ignorant or have issues caused by themselves”
Lack of structure arises from the qualitative and freeform nature of customer feedback, making it difficult to analyze and interpret.
- Real Example (lacking context with spelling errors): “Better than anything in the word!”
Enter Large Language Models (LLMs). LLMs are revolutionizing the analysis of customer feedback. For example, large online retailers are leveraging LLMs to highlight key aspects of product reviews for their consumers, eliminating the need for manual sifting and summarizing. For Meta, the value of customer feedback lies in refining our products and services to meet the evolving needs of our users. By tuning into their voices, we can ensure we’re delivering products and services that truly matter to them.
In this blog post, we’ll explore how qualitative customer feedback data can be just as insightful as traditional quantitative metrics in shaping our products. At Meta’s Reality Labs, we’ve assembled an extensive repository of customer feedback to develop a self-service AI tool — powered by Llama, Meta’s open-sourced LLM — to help understand customer preferences and their influence on product sentiment. We’ll discuss the four components needed to do this (Figure 1) and share examples of how we’ve used customer feedback to enhance our products in the short term and strategically plan for the future.
Data Sourcing: The Importance Of Building a Comprehensive Feedback Repository
The performance of any LLM hinges on the quality and relevance of its data foundation. Even the most sophisticated LLMs can falter, producing “hallucinations” or misleading answers, when they lack sufficient data to answer a question.
The first step to mitigate hallucination risk is to establish an extensive repository of customer feedback that includes:
- A substantial volume of customer feedback: increasing the likelihood of citing relevant information when the LLM is tasked with answering a question.
- A broad range of data sources: ensuring that customer feedback isn’t dominated by a small group of vocal users on a specific platform.
We built our customer feedback repository from a variety of internal and external sources as shown in Table 1.
As illustrated in Table 1, customer feedback can also be characterized by its mutability:
- Immutable feedback is static and unchanging over time (e.g. app reviews)
- Mutable feedback is dynamic and adaptive, evolving as new information becomes available (e.g. social forms of feedback, such as online conversations)
Understanding mutable feedback is crucial for tracking shifts in customer preferences over time. Even the most constructive feedback will eventually become outdated with the passage of time, skewing insights in the here-and-now unless they’re filtered out. For instance, old discussions about “feature requests” that have since been implemented or “bugs” that are now fixed can provide misleading context about the current state of customer sentiment. To address this, incorporating temporal dependence into our data model is essential, allowing us to answer business questions within a specific date range to ensure only the most relevant context is considered.

Retrieval Augmented Generation: Enhancing LLMs with Customer Feedback
What is RAG?
Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG) is a technology that enhances the capabilities of LLMs to provide more accurate and relevant answers (i.e., “responses”) to user questions (i.e., “prompts”). It does this by incorporating external data retrieval into the generation process — in our case, customer feedback. When a user asks a question, RAG retrieves the most relevant customer feedback tailored to their prompt and augments it into the LLM’s context, enabling the model to generate a well-informed final answer.
The process for preparing our customer review dataset for RAG is illustrated in Figure 3:
- Gather Raw Feedback: All customer feedback is assembled in their most granular and raw form.
- Contextualize Feedback: Join any pieces of raw feedback into a contextualized piece of feedback (e.g. elements of a “conversation” as depicted in Figure 2) — and then summarize it.
- Embed Feedback: Generate dense vector representations of the contextualized feedback, commonly referred to as embeddings. Embeddings capture the essence of meaning and context in a body of text, transforming words or phrases into vectors within a high-dimensional space where semantically similar texts naturally cluster together.
- Similarity Search & Retrieval: At runtime, the user’s prompt is matched with relevant customer feedback by comparing its embedding to stored feedback embeddings using cosine similarity, retrieving the top-N most similar matches for consideration.
- Generate Response to User Prompt: The LLM generates a response to the user’s prompt based on the contextually similar customer feedback retrieved.
Mitigating Hallucination Risk
To minimize “hallucinations” where LLMs generate creative, yet inaccurate responses to user prompts, we focus on three key areas:
- Address Out-of-Scope Prompts: Users need to understand what types of questions the LLM can answer. To support this, we’re categorizing the themes of commonly asked questions, tracking the usefulness of their LLM response, and using this information to provide educational “suggestions” to new users.
- Address Poorly Constructed Prompts: Crafting detailed and contextual prompts helps ensure accurate and relevant responses. To support this, we stress the importance of prompt-engineering, of which there are many external courses available.
- Embed Additional Context: For example, we’ve found that, in the case of bug reports, embedding their stack trace, in addition to their freeform description, improves retrieval accuracy for prompts related to the clustering and classification of common bug traits.

Real-World Applications: Using Customer Feedback to Improve Products
As an analytics team, our job is to use data to influence business decisions and drive future strategy. Data isn’t only represented by numbers, it’s also represented by the voices of our users. Here are some of the broader ways we’re using customer feedback within our AI tool to improve products in Reality Labs.
- Bug Deduplication: When a user reports a bug, we assign a unique case number to track and manage the issue. However, it’s common for multiple bugs to be related to a single underlying issue. To optimize our bug ranking and prioritization process, we’re clustering bugs into natural groupings based on their descriptions and stack trace. Mapping bug reports to higher-level categories is helping prioritize and address the most critical areas reported by our users.
- Internal Testing & Quality Assurance: Employees are encouraged to participate in early software testing, resulting in high signal feedback being posted to internal forums, including aspects of reliability, functionality, inclusivity, and accessibility. This data is being summarized in executive reporting, condensing hours of manual effort into minutes.
- Future Use Cases: Our customer feedback represents a diverse range of voices from around the world, spanning multiple age groups, ethnicities, and socioeconomic backgrounds. It also comes from potential future customers. Knowing what people like, dislike, and desire from our Quest & Ray-Ban Meta products today will help us create better devices and experiences for tomorrow.
Bridging Quantitative and Qualitative Insights for Better Products
In this blog, we’ve described how we can take noisy, biased and unstructured customer review data, and transform it into usable product insights for informing short-term decisions and long-term strategies (Figure 5).
Elevating the role of qualitative customer feedback in a discipline traditionally dominated by quantitative metrics may be seen as a bold move. However, we believe it marks a significant shift in how we will analyze and drive product innovation in the near future. Combining the strengths of both quantitative (the “factual what”) and qualitative data (the “contextual why”), can deliver a more nuanced understanding of how users feel towards our products, ultimately bringing about improvements that matter to our users.
As LLMs continue to advance, we envision a future where the convergence between quantitative and qualitative data becomes even stronger — enabling us to correlate metric movements to customer feedback, and tap into richer multimodal data sources to further enrich our understanding of customer preferences.
